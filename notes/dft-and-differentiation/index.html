1 Background

There are plenty of ways to define the Fourier Transform, but here we’ll define the Fourier Transform of a function \(u\in L^2[0,2\pi]\) as \[\begin{align*} \mathcal{F}[u](x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{u} e^{inx}\quad(\star)\\ \text{where}\ \hat{u}_n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}u(x)e^{-inx}dx. \end{align*}\] \(\mathcal{F}[u](x)\) is the Fourier Representation of \(u\), and \(\hat{u}_n\) are the Fourier coefficients. Our truncated Fourier Representation \(\mathcal{F}_N\) is defined as \[ \mathcal{F}_N[u](x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}\hat{u}_n e^{inx}. \]

1.1 Quick Remarks:

If we define an inner product \(\inner{f}{g}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f\bar{g}dx\), the set \(\{e^{inx}: n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\) is orthanormal with respect to the above inner product \[\begin{align*} \inner{e^{imx}}{e^{inx}} &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m-n)x}\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi i(m-n)} e^{i(m-n)x}\bigg|_0^{2\pi} = 0, \quad m\neq n, \\ \inner{e^{inx}}{e^{inx}} &= 1. \end{align*}\] Notice \(\hat{u}_n = \inner{u}{e^{inx}}\), so each of our Fourier terms is a projection of \(u\) onto one of our basis funcitons, i.e. the Fourier basis.

By the completeness of \(L_2\) and the Fourier basis, we get the equality of \((\star)\).

2 Discritizaiton

Suppose we have a function \(f\) on the real interval \([0,2\pi]\), then we can discritize the interval with \(2N + 1\) points where \(x_j = j(\frac{2\pi}{2N+1})\) and \(j\in[2N]\). Additionally, we would like to interpolate the funciton values at each \(x_j\), i.e. \[ f(x_j)=\sum_{|k|\le N} \hat{f}_k e^{ikx_j}. \] Let \(\mathbf E\) be a \((2N+1)\times (2N+1)\) matrix such that \((E)_{j m} = e^{ik x_j}\) where \(j\in[2N]\) corresponds to the row indices and \(m\in[2N]\) corresponds to column indices such that \(m=1\mapsto k= -N,\ \dots,\ m= 2N+1 \mapsto k=N\). Then let \(\mathbf{\hat{f}}=(\hat{f}_{-N},\dots, \hat{f}_N)^T\) and we can represent our interpolation scheme as \[ \mathbf E\mathbf{\hat{f}} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) \] where \(\mathbf E\) is called the Fourier Matrix.

Theorem 1 . \(\mathbf E\) is orthoganal.

Proof. Let our discritizaiton scheme from above hold. We will show \(\mathbf E^* \mathbf E\) is a diagonal matrix. Let \((\mathbf E^*)_{\ell_1,:}\) denote a row of \(\mathbf E^*\) and \((\mathbf E)_{:, \ell_2}\) denote a column of \(\mathbf E\). Then, we have two cases.

\(Case \ 1:\ (\ell_1=\ell_2)\). \[\begin{align*} (\mathbf E^* \mathbf E)_{\ell_1, \ell_1} &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N}e^{-i\ell_1 x_j}e^{i\ell_2 x_j}\\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N}e^{i(-\ell_1+\ell_2)x_j} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N} 1 = 2N+1 \end{align*}\]

\(Case \ 2:\ (\ell_1\ne \ell_2)\) \[\begin{align*} (\mathbf E^* \mathbf E)_{\ell_1, \ell_1} &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N}e^{-i\ell_1 x_j}e^{i\ell_2 x_j}\\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N}e^{i(-\ell_1+\ell_2)x_j} \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{2N}e^{i(-\ell_1+\ell_2)(\frac{2\pi}{2N+1})j}\\ &= \bigg(\frac{1-e^{i(\ell_2-\ell_1)2\pi}}{1-e^{i(-\ell_1+\ell_2)(\frac{2\pi}{2N+1})}} \bigg)=0 \end{align*}\] since \[ |e^{i(-\ell_1+\ell_2)(\frac{2\pi}{2N+1})}|< 1\quad\text{and}\quad e^{i(\ell_2-\ell_1)2\pi}=1. \] Hence \(\mathbf E^* \mathbf E=\frac{1}{2N+1}\mathbf I\).

Corollary 1 (Discrete Fourier Transform) The Discrete Fourier Transform is given by \[ \hat f_k = \frac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{2N} f(x_j)e^{-ikx_j} \]

Proof. Let \(\mathbf E \mathbf{\hat f} = \mathbf f\). Multiplying by \(\mathbf E^*\) on the left yeilds, \[\begin{align*} \mathbf E^* \mathbf E \mathbf{\hat f}&=\mathbf E^* \mathbf f\\ \mathbf{\hat f}&=\frac{1}{2N+1}\mathbf E^* \mathbf f \end{align*}\] which implies the corollary above.

3 Differentiation

In the computational setting differential operators get discritized, i.e. they become matrices. Lets consider a discritizaiton with \(N\) points and \(f\) as above \[\begin{align*} f(x)&\approx \sum_{n\in S_n}\hat{f}_n e^{inx}& S_n=\begin{cases}-\frac{N}{2},\dots,\frac{N}{2}-1&if\ N\ even\\ -\frac{N-1}{2},\dots,\frac{N-1}{2}&if \ N\ odd\end{cases}\\ \hat{f}_n&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} f(x_j)e^{-inx_j}&x_j = j\frac{2\pi}{N}. \end{align*}\] Then \[ \mathbf D_N^{m}f(x_j)\approx \sum_{n\in S_n}\hat{f}_n (in)^m e^{inx}, \] which can be represented as matrix vector multiplicaiton \[\begin{align*} \mathbf D_N^{m}\mathbf f &\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\mathbf E\mathrm{diag}((in)^m, \dots, (in)^m)\mathbf{\hat f}\\ &=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf E\ \mathbf{\mathrm{diag}}((in)^m, \dots, (in)^m)\ \mathbf E^*\mathbf f \end{align*}\] by Corollary 1. Then we get \(\mathbf D_N^{m}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf E\ \mathbf{\mathrm{diag}}((in)^m, \dots, (in)^m)\ \mathbf E^*\), which is the eigen-decomposition of \(\mathbf D_N^{m}\) in the Fourier basis.

Notice the eigenvalue of \(\mathbf D_N^{m}\) are real and negative when \(m\) is even and strictly imaginary when \(m\) is odd.

4 PDEs

Consider the 1-D transport equation \(u_t=u_{x}\), then by discritizing the PDE we get \[ \frac{d}{dt}\overrightarrow{u}(t)=\mathbf D_N\overrightarrow{u}(t). \] Indeed \(\mathbf D_N\) has complex eigenvalues in the Fourier basis. Further, \[\begin{align*} \mathbf D_N \mathbf D_N^* &=\frac{1}{N^2}\mathbf E\ \mathbf{\mathrm{diag}}((in)^m, \dots, (in)^m)\ \mathbf E^*(\mathbf E\ \mathbf{\mathrm{diag}}((in)^m, \dots, (in)^m)\ \mathbf E^*)^*\\ &=\mathbf I \end{align*}\] so let \(\mathbf x, \mathbf y\in\mathbb{R}^N\), then \(\|\mathbf D_N \mathbf x -\mathbf D_N \mathbf y\|=\|\mathbf D_N(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\|=\|\mathbf x-\mathbf y\|\). Hence, as an operator \(\mathbf D_N\) preserves rigid motions in euclidian space, as expected by the transport equation.

Now consider the 1-D diffusion equation \(u_t=u_{xx}\), then by discritizing the PDE we get \[ \frac{d}{dt}\overrightarrow{u}(t)=\mathbf D^2_N\overrightarrow{u}(t). \] Since \(\mathbf D^2_N\) has real negative eigenvalues, from the ODE perspective we can think of the PDE as decaying to a fixed point in the Fourier basis, i.e. the \(k=0\) wavenumber.
